# The story

This game is inspired in part by a true story. The opening, up to the operation, is the author's
own memory. Everything after it, the escape through the hospital, is invented.

## What happened

A children's hospital in Russia, around 1997. He was about five. He was in a ward room with
his mother, playing on the floor, in a good mood. Doctors came in. His mother understood he
needed an operation and handed him over. He cried and asked for her. He was carried down a
long hallway to a room where it was done with no general anaesthetic: a doctor held him on her
lap in a chair, his eyes were covered with cloth, and he was awake for all of it. He does not
remember pain. He remembers a great deal of blood. It stayed with him for a long time.

The game follows the memory up to the chair. Then it leaves it: the child gets away.

## It was not unusual

Nobody knows now which operation it was. What he remembers matches how adenoids, and often
tonsils, were removed from children in Soviet and 1990s Russian clinics. This is what
Russian-language sources say, each checked against the page it comes from:

- **It was done awake.** An ear, nose and throat surgeon writes that adenoids used to be
  removed "without anaesthesia, practically alive", with an adenotome, a curved knife shaped
  like a loop. The surgeon worked blind and checked the result with a finger.
  ([SM-Clinic, on Habr](https://habr.com/ru/companies/smclinic/articles/990100/))
- **The child was held or tied.** A Moscow clinic describes the old method in two sentences:
  "They drip anaesthetic into the nose and tie the child down. It looks like medieval
  torture." It adds: "A lot of blood, a lot of stress, little effect."
  ([medicina.ru](https://www.medicina.ru/press-tsentr/statyi/operatsiya-po-udaleniyu-adenoidov-eto-ne-tragediya/))
- **People remember it the same way.** The surgeon's article opens with a mother recalling
  her own childhood operation: a nurse tied her to a chair, the sheets were soaked in blood,
  and she remembers the metallic taste of it. Another account describes an orange
  leatherette chair and being wrapped from head to foot in a rubber apron with his arms
  underneath. ([LiveJournal](https://masterok.livejournal.com/6814032.html))
- **Why.** The only reason we found stated is second-hand: it was held that a child bearing
  a couple of minutes of pain was safer than the general anaesthesia of that time. The
  surgeon's article says only that anaesthesia has since become safer and easier to get.
- **Now.** It is done under general anaesthesia with an endoscope.

What these sources do not explain is why the child sat upright on someone's lap. Our guess,
and it is only a guess: a child who is awake, upright and leaning forward bleeds out of the
mouth instead of into the airway, and a lap and a pair of arms hold a five-year-old still for
the minute it takes.

Whether he was given anything for the pain, nobody can say. Numbing drops or an injection
were part of the method as described; he remembers no pain, and he was five.

This is not told to blame anyone. The people in that room were doing what they had been
taught was normal. It is told because a great many children went through it, and almost
nobody talks about it.

## Like a film

The game is meant to play like a film you are inside: chapters with title cards, no health
bars or score, long quiet stretches, sound doing most of the work, and stretches where you
can only look.

After the ending, the text of "It was not unusual" is shown to the player, with the sources.

## The people

- **Mom.** Blonde, straight hair past her shoulders with a fringe, blue eyes. A soft sweater,
  jeans, house shoes. Calm. She is the only warm thing
  in the building and the thing the whole escape points at.
- **The doctors.** Seen the way a child sees them. At the door they are ordinary, polite
  people in white. From the moment he is taken out of his mother's arms they begin to change,
  too slowly to catch: the one walking behind grows taller, their necks and faces lengthen,
  their eyes open too wide and stop blinking, and a smile spreads and stays. By the escape
  they are too tall, their faces are gone behind the mask and the mirror, and they call his
  name softly. They never run.
- **The child.** You. Never seen, except his hands.

## The place

- **The ward room.** One bed, an old metal one. Walls painted in bright children's colours,
  drawings pinned up, toys on the floor, daylight. The most colourful place in the game.
- **Everywhere else.** Two-tone oil-painted walls, linoleum, enamel dishes, a glass syringe
  steriliser, a quartz lamp, a radio somewhere down the hall.

## Rules of the escape

- **Small.** Crawl under beds, gurneys and counters and through gaps adults cannot follow.
- **Hide.** Under a bed or in a cupboard a doctor walks past, unless they saw you go in.
- **Toys, not weapons.** A squeaky toy thrown down a hall pulls a doctor to the noise.
- **Sound gives you away.** Running is loud, crying is louder. Walking softly is slow.
- **Being caught** is being picked up and carried back towards the chair, not dying. You can
  wriggle free only so many times.

## How much it shows

Decided: the game is aimed at players of 16 and over.

- **Blood is shown,** and plenty of it where the story calls for it: soaking through the
  cloth over his eyes, on gowns and gloves, in an enamel dish, on the floor, a trail down
  the corridor.
- **The cutting is never shown.** No instruments in a child's mouth, no wounds on a child's
  body. His eyes were covered; the player sees what he saw.
- **A warning at the start** says what the game is: inspired in part by a true story, a child's
  medical procedure, blood and distress.
- **A "less blood" setting** for players who want the story without the red.

## Voices

Everyone speaks English, with ordinary American accents. Mom has lines in the ward; the
doctors will have a few, said gently, which is worse.

## The clock

There is a clock on the ward wall. It starts at four minutes to ten. Whatever the player is
doing, at ten the door opens. Mom knows; she glances at the door, and at the clock.

Beyond the door the same building has no colour in it: stained brick, bare concrete, one cold
light a long way down the hall. Opening the door is the cut from the first half of the story
to the second.

## Build order

1. The ward room and the shape sorter, at child height. (Built.)
2. The handover and the corridor, one unbroken carried walk. (Built.)
3. The chair, done with cloth, sound and colour only.
4. The escape in the hospital map: crawling, hiding, doctors that search.
5. Mom, the ending, and the closing text.
